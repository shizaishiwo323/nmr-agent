Least-squares and linear programming 
algorithms for the interpretation 
of NMR relax- 
ation data in terms of a spectrum of relaxation times are presented. These stable methods 
are noniterative 
and avoid all of the difficulties associated with nonlinear 
optimization 
schemes. Moreover, they are not restricted by any assumptions which limit the number 
of possible relaxation times. The inherent nonuniqueness 
due to the finite number of 
inaccurate measurements means that a variety of discrete and continuous spectra exist 
which fit the data equally well. For a complete interpretation 
the range of possible solu- 
tions must be examined. The algorithms discussed explore the space of acceptable solu- 
tions by constructing relaxation spectra from a continuum 
of models ranging from a few 
isolated delta functions to a continuous 
distribution. 
Alternatively, 
the interpreter can 
choose that class of spectrum (discrete or continuous) 
which best represents the physical 
system under investigation and so enhance the reliability of the interpretation. 
This flex- 
ibility is essential in order to avoid the pitfalls of interpreting 
a single spectrum returned 
by a single algorithm. 
In addition, the linear programming 
approach reduces the non- 
uniqueness by constructing unique information 
in the form of bounds on all spectra 
which satisfy the observations. These bounds and the corresponding 
extreme models are 
necessary for any quantitative determination 
of how spectral peaks change in amplitude 
or position with a change in the physical system.

of time constants 
is a nontrivial 
problem. Nevertheless, the quantitative 
interpretation 
of NMR relax-

of the “true” 
distribution 
of relaxation times present in the sample.

for this study comes from work by our group on proton magnetic

The most common 
approach to the analysis of multiexponential 
relaxation is to 
fit the decay curves with the smallest number 
of discrete exponential 
terms which 
provide a satisfactory representation 
of the experimental 
data. For a variety of rea-

nential components 
or when any two time constants are similar. Another approach 
is to analyze the data in terms of a continuous 
distribution 
of relaxation times. This 
has been done both as a nonlinear 
problem by, for example, Provencher (3) and 
Smith and Nichols (4)) and as a linear problem by Provencher (5, 6), Kroeker and 
Henkelman 
( 7)) and Kroeker, Stewart, Bronskill, and Henkelman 
(8). The continu- 
ous approach has the very desirable advantage of requiring few a priori assumptions 
about the relaxation behavior and it usually provides a more stable fit to the experi- 
mental data. 
The aim of this paper is to discuss critically the application 
of linear inverse tech- 
niques to characterize decay curves in terms of discrete or continuous 
distributions 
of relaxation times. In particular, we investigate the nonuniqueness 
inherent in this 
problem. We demonstrate 
that a wide range of statistically acceptable, but quantita- 
tively very different, 
solutions exist for multiexponential 
decay curves. We present 
algorithms 
which explore this space of acceptable models and appraise the non- 
uniqueness by finding bounds on spectra over relaxation time intervals. 
To illustrate the application 
of these techniques, we use simulated decay curves 
from both continuous 
and discrete relaxation time distributions. 
We also analyze an 
actual CPMG decay curve from a western red cedar sapwood sample.

CONCLUSIONS 
The least-squares and linear programming 
algorithms presented here solve the lin- 
ear integral Eq. [ 11 for a discrete or smooth spectrum of relaxation times s( T) satisfy- 
ing the data to within their errors. These stable methods require no starting model, 
no iterative solution, and no restrictive assumptions limiting the spectrum to a few 
delta functions. All the approaches generate minimum 
structure models, whether a 
few isolated delta functions or a smoothly varying profile. This philosophy of parsi- 
mony (5, 16, 20) prevents generation of spurious features not required by the data 
and so increases confidence in the resulting structure. Moreover, 
both methods can 
incorporate 
additional 
equality or inequality 
constraints on any linear combination 
of the spectral amplitudes. 
These construction 
methods must be supplemented 
by techniques which appraise 
the extent of the nonuniqueness. 
Figures 4 and 6, for example, show that discrete or 
smooth models fit data from both discrete and smooth spectra equally well. Figure 9 
demonstrates 
that restricting models to be smooth still allows substantial variation. 
Using the LP method to minimize 
and maximize 
the objective function 
generates 
different information 
in the form of bounds on the set of all spectra which fit the data.

This appraisal aspect of LP is not concerned with the features of any one model but 
with features common 
to all acceptable spectra. Examination 
of the spectra corre-

permitted 
by 
the data. 
In addition, the NNLS algorithm answers the question of existence by finding that 
s( T) with the minimum 
least-squares misfit. If the minimum 
value is unacceptably 
high then the relaxation mechanism is different or more complex than the equation 
being used to represent the physical system ( Eq. [ I], for example). 
Existence and the construction 
and appraisal methods presented here depend on 
obtaining good estimates of the standard deviations ui. Extra experimental 
effort de- 
voted to determining 
these values leads to rich rewards in the amount of information 
returned. Data without 
error bars are virtually meaningless because there is no mea- 
sure of confidence in their values. This translates to no confidence in the determina- 
tion of what possible variations are permitted 
by the data and which spectrum is 
optimal.
